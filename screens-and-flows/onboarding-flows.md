# Onboarding Flows

Onboarding earns the user's first impression: give a reason to start, make setup effortless, and be honest about commitment.

## When to use

- Designing welcome and first-run screens.
- Collecting preferences or profile data during setup.
- Asking for notification permission or reminders.
- Explaining a free trial or subscription.

General form rules: `forms-and-inputs`. Sign-up screens: `auth-screens`. Tone and copy: `ux-writing-microcopy`. Peak-End logic: `ux-laws`.

## 1. Make the value specific

- Give people a reason to sign up. Open with situations the user recognises (for example venting, or making sense of a confusing message) rather than a generic description.
- Lead with a situation your user recognises, then show the benefit before asking for sign-up.

## 2. Make setup feel like a conversation

- Carry the user's answers forward: use the name they just gave in the next question ("hey Alex, welcome in. when do we get to celebrate you?").
- Playful copy and artwork make each step feel part of one welcome.
- Use a voice that fits your audience.

## 3. Lower the effort to answer

- **Tap to answer**: concrete options ("stress less") are easier than a blank text response. Allow a "something else" option and say how many can be chosen.
- **Show what changed**: selected options get a color fill and a check mark.
- **Keep a way through**: show a progress bar (for example "5/5"), a back control and a Skip action to give context and control.
- One question per screen, a large Next button; use appropriate inputs (range slider, picker).
- Collect preferences early so settings are personalised from the start and need less digging later. The more data you collect up front, the less tedious it is to fill in settings later (see `settings-and-profile`).
- Explain why sensitive data is needed.

## 4. Give control over reminders

- Tie reminders to a benefit ("you're setting aside space for yourself").
- Let users choose timing: morning, day and evening each with its own time and switch.
- Allow "later": put "Set up later" beside the primary action so users can continue without committing to notifications.

## 5. Make the trial predictable

- Explain a free trial as a short timeline: Today (access starts), Day 6 (reminder before it ends), Day 7 (trial ends, user is charged).
- Keep the price and billing terms close to the action button.
- Show social proof (a short review) and a "view all plans" link.

## 6. Keep the whole offer consistent

- Do not label access as a "free launch gift" on one screen and a trial-then-charge on another. Users should know which offer applies to them and what happens when.

## Review questions

1. A reason to start - is the benefit clear before sign-up?
2. A human tone - does the wording suit your audience?
3. Easier answers - can people tap, skip and see progress?
4. Reminder control - can they choose a time or come back later?
5. A predictable offer - are access, cost and timing consistent?

Borrow the ideas, then test them with your users.
